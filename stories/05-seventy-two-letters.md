# Seventy-Two Letters

In an alternative history where the science of names—of utterances that could animate matter—had developed instead of modern chemistry and biology, Robert Stratton worked as a nomenclator, one who devised the true names that brought automata to life.

The year was 1851, and England was powered not by steam and coal, but by automata: mechanical laborers animated by carefully crafted names written on parchment and placed within their forms. But Stratton had discovered a terrible truth that threatened the very foundation of human civilization.

Humanity was not reproducing naturally, as people believed. Instead, every human being was the product of nested homunculi—tiny preformed humans that unfolded during development. But the supply of these homunculi was finite, decreasing with each generation. Humanity had perhaps five generations left before extinction.

Working with anatomist Dr. Ashbourne and the ingenious duchess Lady Maitland, Stratton sought to find a solution using the science of nomenclature. Could they create artificial wombs powered by names? Could they discover the true name that would allow human reproduction to continue?

"Seventy-Two Letters" is set in a world where Jewish Kabbalistic concepts of creation through language have become the basis of an entire scientific tradition. Chiang creates a richly detailed alternative Victorian England where golems work in factories and the power of names drives technological progress.

The story explores themes of creation, reproduction, and the power of language to shape reality, while also examining issues of class, gender, and social progress in a Victorian setting where magical principles have replaced natural science.
